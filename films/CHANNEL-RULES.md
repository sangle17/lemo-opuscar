# Channel rules: how every film for the channel is made

The user's YouTube channel and Facebook Page ("Our Encyclopedia") turn encyclopedia pages into short films for a US/EU audience, in English. These rules sit on top of `AGENTS.md`, `DIRECTOR.md` and the chosen `STYLE.md`. **The style decides the look; these rules decide the topic, the hook, the script, the facts, the titles and the posting.** If a rule here ever conflicts with `STYLE.md`, the style wins.

The other channel docs:
- [`DIRECTING-PLAYBOOK.md`](DIRECTING-PLAYBOOK.md): how to think while directing, the evidence behind it, and the checklist every film runs.
- [`FIRST-FRAME.md`](FIRST-FRAME.md): the first 3 seconds.
- [`LONG-FORM.md`](LONG-FORM.md): films of 12–20 min.
- `research/`: the studies these rules come from.

New channel lessons go into these files, not into an agent's private memory, so every tool and session can reuse them (user, 2026-10-31). Write down what was learned, with the evidence and the date.

## Strategy: long films earn, Shorts find the stories

Decided 2026-11-02 (user: "ok" to strategy 2 of three: Shorts as main income, Shorts feeding long films, or both equally).

- **Long films are the business.** Shorts pay roughly $0.03–0.15 per 1,000 views; long history and geography videos for a US/EU audience often pay $3–10 (rough public creator figures, not yet checked against our own Studio). Monetising on Shorts alone needs 10M Shorts views in 90 days, and Shorts views don't count toward the 4,000 long-form watch hours. Our Shorts outside Korea sat at 73–362 YouTube views in the week of 26 Oct. Facebook Reels (Korea: 174,025 plays) may pay a bonus, but no plan rests on it.
- **Shorts are the daily test and the shop window.** One a day (§8), each one testing a story family. A Short wins on swipe rate, retention, new subscribers and comments that ask for more ("part 2", "what happened to…"), not on views alone.
- **Shorts viewers don't follow to long films by themselves.** So what a Short collects is evidence of what people want, not just subscribers. A long film's topic comes from a family whose Shorts won, or whose comments asked what the Short couldn't answer, or a story flagged as over ~1:30 (§2). Shorts linked to a long film follow §3.
- **Rhythm:** Shorts every day; one long film every 2–3 weeks, built only from winning families.
- **Judge each on its own goal.** Shorts: swipe rate, retention, subscribers. Long films: watch hours, CTR per traffic source, average view duration, RPM (§12).
- **Review after 3–4 long films.** If long films built from winners still stall, fix their packaging or pacing first. If 12–20 min can't be held every 2–3 weeks, test 8–10 min long films before dropping the strategy.

## 0. Picking the topic

### The niche: "How did they get that?"

Decided 2026-11-03 (user). This replaces "anything in the book". Every film answers one repeatable question about a piece of land: **who took, bought, swapped, shared or lost it, and why it still looks that way today.** Examples: territory sales and swaps, land lost to stay free, exclaves and enclaves, shared islands, disputed rocks, lines drawn on a map, empty or unclaimed land. A subscriber should always know what the next video will be.

- **Each film follows the same three beats:**
  - **the odd fact** (the hook: "a piece of Germany belongs to Russia");
  - **how it happened** (two sides acting: a war, a deal, a sale, a trick, a vote);
  - **why it's still that way** (the payoff today).

  "Who owns it" is only the hook; "how they got it" is the film.
- **In:** any country or era, as long as the story turns on who controls a piece of land.
- **Out:** general history (wars, kings, empires) unless the land changing hands is the story; country trivia; animals; science; name origins on their own. 22 off-niche stories were deleted from the Hook Bank on 2026-11-03.
- **Where we can win:** the less famous deals told with real evidence (the treaty, the price, the map before and after). The famous ones (Hawaii, Puerto Rico, Alaska, Kaliningrad, Texas as an overview) already have 2–7M-view films from big channels. Tell those only through one moment the big channels skipped.
- **Evidence:** `research/niche-research-2026-11-03.md` (our analytics, YouTube autocomplete, Google Trends, 17 competitor channels).
- **Crowding:** autocomplete often repeats a big channel's own title, so it doesn't prove a gap. Search the phrase and check the top results every time.
- **Geography belongs in the niche when nature moves the line** (user, 2026-11-04). Pure geography (the tallest mountain, a lake called a sea) stays out. In: a river shifts and the border moves with it (the Chamizal), a melting glacier moves the Italy–Switzerland line, a new volcanic island rises and someone has to own it, the seabed under the Arctic. The viewer gets real geography plus the niche question anyone understands: "so whose is it now?"

Inside the niche, these rules decide which *story* we tell. **Story first, page second** (user, 2026-10-31: "most of our video didn't go that way"). Start from a story a stranger would want to hear, then use the page as its anchor and fact source; never list the page's facts and hunt for a hook. Off-page stories about a page keyword are fine.

Built on 2026-10-31 from our own analytics, The Historian's Den (Reels with 0.8–1.7M views), viewer comments on a 500K-subscriber channel, and 11,550 top comments under 77 history and geography Shorts with 1–28M views (`research/audience-questions-2026-10-31.md`).

### Where leads come from

1. **Questions viewers already ask.** Comments under big history and geography Shorts, ranked by likes: "I always wondered why…", "what happened to…", "ain't there a man who…", "we need a part 2". yt-dlp downloads them (`--write-comments`, top sort).
2. **Outliers elsewhere.** A Short or Reel on the story that got far more views than its channel's usual.
3. **What adults half-remember from school.** Viewers met the famous thing at school, forgot the details, and now want the question school never answered. "Your channel answers SO MANY questions we have as adults"; "I'm visiting your channel with my notebook and pen… to write down a lot of facts which I read earlier in my school but forgot"; "Been scratching my head since elementary school on why it's called Greenland" (693 likes).
4. **Our own winners.** Stories in the same family as a hit. Korea's flagpoles were a rivalry between two sides that know each other well.
5. **The page's keywords**, and the date (Halloween → Dracula).

### First: what does the Short bring the viewer?

This comes before everything else (user, 2026-10-31: "the priority is what does the short bring to the viewers"). Write it in one sentence: **after watching, the viewer knows or understands ___.** It should be something they'd tell a friend or write down, not trivia they forget by the next swipe. The strongest kinds:
- **An answer to a question they already carry** ("why is it called Greenland?").
- **A new way to see something familiar,** such as a border, a map or a famous person (Wagah: the rival armies' show of hostility ended every evening in a handshake, until 2025).
- **A real thing they've never seen:** the object, the face, the sound.

Viewers thank channels for exactly this: "Your channel answers SO MANY questions we have as adults"; "write down a lot of facts which I read earlier in my school but forgot". If the sentence is weak, no hook or famous name saves the Short. A neat fact that changes nothing for the viewer (our Austrian village "only reachable through Germany": 90 plays) isn't enough.

### Who has to care: the US/EU viewer

Decided 2026-11-04 (user: "If u was american will u watch it. Be honest."). The long films earn from US/EU viewers, and a Short's winners pick the long films, so **pick for what a US/EU viewer wants to know**, inside the niche. Not broadly: a Short that wins only in one far-off country tells us nothing about the long films' audience.

- **A Short's audience follows its subject.** Our country data, last 28 days to 2026-11-04 (YouTube Analytics API, per video): the Philippine warship went 866 Philippines / 102 US; Wagah 1,032 Indonesia (about 7 s watched per view, against about 20 s in the US); Mexico's oil island 231 Mexico / 159 US; Austria-Hungary 66 Austria; the Everest mystery 132 US / 43 UK / 16 Australia. Those two local stories made up most of the channel's Shorts views in the Philippines (27.6%) and Indonesia (22.2%); without them the US leads. The post time wasn't the cause: Wagah went out at 23:00 Vietnam time, the US slot. About 15 Shorts, so a signal, not proof.
- **Universal stories travel; local ones stay home.** Korea's flagpoles spread across six countries with few Korean viewers, because two rivals racing needs no background. A story that needs the viewer to care about one country's dispute first stays in that region.
- **The answer must matter to them, not only the hook.** A hook buys about 3 seconds; our Reels lose most viewers between 3 and 15 s (§12), where the viewer decides whether they want the answer. "Why does Denmark own Greenland?" works because Americans already ask it (autocomplete). "How did France get Laos?" (Paknam, 2026-11-04) answers a question few Americans carry: a gunboat hook might hold them 3 s longer, but they'd leave once it turned to Siam. The topic decides; the hook is craft on top.
- **A Western country on one side isn't enough on its own.** The hook has to stand on something a US/EU viewer already knows or feels in a second: a famous place or country, a sale or a trick, a dramatic act. A little-known place can be the payoff, never the opening.
- **The mix:** about 70–80% of Shorts universal or US/EU-anchored (a Western country as a side *and* a hook they get; any country's story told through a universal act). Stories where both sides are little-known in the West only when the hook is really strong (Korea's flagpoles). Stories about Vietnam itself may suit the Vietnamese channel test better.
- **The pick test, three questions:** is it land changing hands? Will a US/EU viewer care about the **answer**? Do people already ask it (autocomplete, comments, an outlier)?

### Then it must pass all five

1. **"Wait, what?"** They would need to know what happened: a person or two sides, an action that sounds impossible, one open question. Models: the North/South Korea flagpole race, Onoda fighting on until 1974, the Hasanlu "lovers", the forest of stakes that turned back a sultan.
2. **A payoff that a stranger fully understands in the length the story needs** (§2), ideally a minute or less. A story that needs more than about 1:30 is a long-video candidate, not one to cut (§2). One story with a twist or answer at the end, nothing missing that they'd need to follow it. A setup with no payoff gets "'It's insane' is how this video ends? I was just getting interested". If it needs three background steps first, it's a long film, not a Short.
3. **Real evidence exists**, with a commercial licence: a photo of the thing or the place, the document, the object, the sound. Without it the film is only drawings, and viewers notice ("But you don't have a sample of this sound?", 48,000 likes).
4. **Literally true and checkable** (§10). Contested history needs the debate in the script, or a different story. Errors become the top comment ("How many mistakes can you cram into 30 seconds?", 3,800 likes).
5. **Not a fight.** No stories whose comments turn into religious, ethnic or live-war fights (religious majorities, Kashmir). A rivalry with a wink is the opposite, and good (flagpoles, the whisky war, Wagah's high kicks). Where a live conflict is part of the story, keep the script neutral and the first comment about the subject, not about sides.

### What makes a lead stronger

- **Two sides acting against each other** (week of 26 Oct, read 2026-11-01). The strongest type we have: two countries or sides doing something to each other that you can see (building, landing, kicking, claiming). All four top Shorts of the week were this type: Korea flagpole race (YouTube 1,316 / Facebook 174,025), the Philippine warship on the reef against China (1,217 / 362), Mexico's oil island against the US (790), Wagah high kicks (728 on YouTube in 1.5 days). One odd place or one lone person stayed under 150 on YouTube: Jungholz 92, Neutral Moresnet 140, Ötzi 13, Argentina's Antarctic birth 23, and Onoda 92 despite the same Asian audience as the warship. A border oddity with nobody acting (an enclave, a shared island, a line through a town) is not this type. Small samples, one week: keep checking it.
- **An anchor:** a US/EU viewer recognises the subject in one second, by a famous name (Korea, WWII, Paris, Everest, Cleopatra) or an everyday human thing (a hug, a shoe, a heart kept in a desk). It helps a Short get noticed: every big hit in the comment study had one (Germany in WW2 28M, Hiroshima 28M, the Pope 20M, Cleopatra 17M, Greenland 7.8M). It helps reach; it doesn't replace what the viewer takes home.
- **Proven demand:** an outlier elsewhere, a question in the comments with hundreds of likes, YouTube autocomplete or Google Trends. Shorts get almost no views from search (Korea: 5.7% of views from search; the rest from the feed), so look for proof that people want the story, not for keywords. If many big channels already have it, it's crowded: take a fresher angle (our real evidence, a turn they skipped). Landscape films are the exception: their searchable keyword goes first in the title (§6).
- **Room for a comment:** two sides or countries people can cheer for, a punchline moment. Most top comments are jokes with countries as characters ("🇮🇹: can I have some?", 12,000 likes).
- **Something real to see or hear** that the viewer has never seen: the object, the face, the sound.
- **"What did X really look like?"**: a face built from a skull or other evidence (History with Dave: Cleopatra 17M, a "Polish vampire" 3.9M).
- **Timely:** in the news this week, or on a date (Halloween).

A lead with no sign of demand isn't banned, but it needs a strong "wait, what?" to make up for it.

### Write it down

**At pick time, not after** (user, 2026-11-01: "we should have a reason to make a video with the hook"). No story goes on the Week board, as a named card, a slot or a Short cut from a long film, until this pick sheet is written in the card's notes and the Hook Bank row: the niche fit (who got which land and how, in one line), the takeaway, **why a US/EU viewer would want the answer** (one line; "a Western country is one of the sides" alone isn't a reason), the "wait, what?", the five checks (pass or fail, one line each), the strengtheners it has, and the demand evidence actually checked (YouTube autocomplete, a comment with its likes, an outlier with its views). "It's from our long film" or "the assets are ready" is never the reason; it only makes a story that already passes cheaper. A story that fails a check doesn't go on the board.

Then, in the film's `TREATMENT.md`, before the script, record:
- **the niche fit**: who got which land, how, and why it's still that way;
- **what the viewer takes home**, in one sentence;
- the "wait, what?";
- the payoff in one line;
- the real evidence (file and licence);
- the anchor, if there is one, and the demand evidence (links and numbers);
- the variety check (§11.9): not the same subject or hook formula as the last few films.

## 1. Choosing the shape

Every Short has a hook, real value (the viewer learns something true and worth knowing, never "bullshit content") and a point of view. **The shape is chosen per subject**; no shape fits every story (user, 2026-10-24). Say which one and why when showing the script. Mixing is fine.

| Shape | When it fits | Order |
|---|---|---|
| **Story** | One event that is itself the value | Hook → what happened → turn → payoff → POV |
| **Main point + fun fact** | A place or thing worth explaining, with a surprising story attached (Tierra del Fuego + the beavers) | Hook with the fun fact → what the subject is → the fun fact, grown out of it → tie-back + POV |
| **Fun fact with numbers** | One striking fact that carries itself | The fact → the details and numbers that make it vivid → POV |
| **Quiz** | Only when the question has a fairly guessable answer (a place, a record, a number) | Question → hints → reveal → POV |

**No "take a guess".** Never ask the viewer to guess in the voice ("Take a guess", "Quick guess", "Where would you put it?"), in any shape (user, 2026-10-31: it isn't good for delivery). A real question can still open a gap ("So where did it go?"); the story answers it straight away, with no pause held for a guess.

**Source: the user's encyclopedia page.** Facts are free to use; the book's sentences and pictures are not. Retell in our own words, with drawn visuals or open-licence material.

## 2. The Short (vertical 9:16)

- **Length: the content decides, never a target** (user, 2026-10-31: "short or long is not the problem, the problem is the content"). Make the topic as good as it can be, then let it run as long as it needs. Never cut a film to hit a time, never pad it, never speed up the voice.
  - **Before writing, list what a stranger needs to fully understand the story** (often the cause, what happened and how it ended) and keep all of it. The Hans Island 30 s cut dropped the 1973 treaty gap that started the fight and was removed; a 3:42 video on the same story was "super clear" because it gave the cause, the happening and the resolution, with evidence and a link to today.
  - **Prefer topics that deliver fast.** When something new can be learned whole in a minute or less, people love it: it costs them little, it's fun, and they want the next one. So when choosing between stories, favour the one a stranger can fully understand quickly; don't take a big story and squeeze it.
  - **About 1:30 is a signal, not a cap** (user, 2026-10-31). A story that needs more than about 1:30 to tell well has potential as a long video (§4, `LONG-FORM.md`): say so to the user and note it in the Hook Bank. Never cut it down to fit.
- **Hook in the first 3 s**, spoken from frame 1 over a strong, moving image. No title card, no fade from black, no "Did you know…", no "In this video". The first frame's rules: [`FIRST-FRAME.md`](FIRST-FRAME.md).
- **One point-of-view line** in the narrator's voice. A loop back into the first line only if it comes naturally.
- **The look belongs to the style.** `STYLE.md` and the style's demo set the bar for everything visual: composition, characters, motion, camera, type, and how much text is on screen. These rules never add title cards, big captions, stamps or labels on top (user, 2026-10-24: the rules had turned every style into text on flat cards). Build real scenes at the quality of the demo.
- **Real evidence first; draw only what a still can't tell** (user, 2026-10-31). Show the real thing whenever a commercially usable one exists: a photo of the actual object or place, the document, a demonstration, a real portrait of every named person. If the story is about a sound, play the real sound. Draw and animate only what no single picture shows: a chain of actions, an emotion, a process. Real images sit inside the style's look, never as cards on top. **If real footage or photos exist of the spectacle, the spectacle is real.** The Wagah Short (2026-10-31) drew the high kicks as cartoon soldiers while sharp photos and video of the real kicks existed; the user found the art "weird", and the cut was rebuilt with real material only. Korea worked drawn because no photo shows two flagpoles growing in turn.
- **Use the style's own engine.** Copy the demo's renderer (print shader, stroke engine, brush engine) into the project and draw with it. Never swap it for a quicker stack (plain SVG, Remotion shapes, flat fills with a texture on top). The Tierra del Fuego "watercolour" and the Argentina Risograph cuts (2026-10-24) became flat vector that way.
- **Compose for the vertical frame.** Stage every shot for 9:16 and fill the frame; never a small subject in the top half over empty ground.
- **Platform limits:**
  - Keep the bottom ~15% free of important detail (the Shorts UI).
  - Deliver captions as an `.srt` file.

## 3. Several Shorts on one subject

- **Each Short stands alone,** with its own hook, its own first frame and its own ending. Never cut-downs of the same Short, never the same opening footage, never the same hook formula twice (YouTube's "inauthentic content" rule targets near-duplicates; Meta cuts the reach of duplicates).
- Shorts may share a film's art, voice engine and score. They may reuse a long film's assets a week after it, if the topic stands alone.
- **A Short linked to a long film never tells the long film's own story** (user, 2026-11-02: "if it succeeds in drawing viewers to the long one, they leave anyway because of the repeating content"). The Short's job is to send people to the long film, and a viewer who has just seen the payoff leaves the long film when it gets there. So a Short with "Related video" set to a long film tells either:
  - **a story the long film leaves out** (its "Cut" list: a side story, a person, a later or earlier event), with the long film as the natural next question; or
  - **the long film's question without its answer** (a teaser that stops where the long film starts).

  Never one of its chapters retold, and never its midpoint, twist or answer. Example: the Greenland long film's chapter 5 (Norway's flag, Ihlen's promise, the 1933 court) is the long film's answer, so a Short on it (`films/greenland-short-norway-promise`, 2026-11-02) repeats the film it links to. Check this at pick time, in the pick sheet: "Is this story told in the long film? Which chapter?"
- Make a Short only when its hook passes the gate (§11). Two strong Shorts beat three where one is padding.
- Every Short with a long version sets "Related video" to it.
- Interleave subjects so the channel never shows one topic for a week straight.

## 4. Landscape and long films

Long films (12–20 min) follow [`LONG-FORM.md`](LONG-FORM.md). A short landscape cut (16:9, 2:30–3:00, about 400 words) needs **one spine**, decided before writing:
- **One story:** the pages share one question ("Why were only 2 African countries free in 1914?"). Use the outline below.
- **Quiz round:** an overview page with many unrelated facts. "N questions about Asia most people get wrong. Keep score." Each chapter: question → guess pause (2–3 s, on-screen countdown) → answer → one deeper fact the Short didn't have. End on the score bands and "What was your score?". Never the Shorts stitched together.
- **Neither fits:** Shorts only. Don't force a scattered long video.

| Time | Chapter | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | **Hook + promise** | The question, then "by the end you'll know X". The first 10 s deliver what the title and thumbnail promised. |
| 0:10 | **Setup** | What the subject is, with a sense of scale. |
| 0:40 | **Why the obvious answer is wrong** | The clues. |
| ~1:20 | **The answer** (about 60%) | The reveal. |
| 1:50 | **The real story** | Why and how, with a person, a date, a moment. |
| 2:30 | **Ending** | Back to the opening image, then a new question and a comment prompt. |

Each chapter opens with a small re-hook. Chapters go in the description.

## 5. Script rules

- **One fact per sentence,** about 12 words at most. Enthusiastic, informal delivery.
- **Talk like a person** (user, 2026-10-23). Never number points in the voice or on screen ("Clue one…"). Read every line aloud; if you wouldn't say it to a friend, rewrite it.
- **Pauses are part of the script** (user, 2026-10-23: "pausing is an art"). Mark them:
  - 0.2–0.3 s between sentences;
  - about 0.45 s between beats (`[BEAT]`);
  - about 0.65 s after a surprising number or the hook (`[HOLD]`).

  Tightened 2026-11-03 (Pheasant Island): at 0.6 / 0.8 s, 11.8 s of a 77 s voice was silence and the user felt it slow; with `am_fenrir` at 1.1 and these pauses the film ran at 189 wpm and was "good now". Aim for about 185–190 wpm overall.

  The voice is silent in a pause, but the picture keeps moving and the music carries the gap. Trim words to fit, never the pauses.
- **Things happening, not a list.** A chain of actions (someone decides, sails, signs, flies…), with the key number or reveal at a turn, where an action changes.
- **After the hook, move forward, never back.** The second beat advances what the hook promised. Background slips in inside the action ("When the South's pole hit 100 m in 1982…"), never as a step back into history. The Korea Short kept 68.7% past the swipe, then lost about 40% of viewers between 0:05 and 0:12, exactly over a rhetorical question ("Why would two countries care…?") and "In 1953, the fighting stops at a line." From 0:12 the curve stayed almost flat.
- **Every fact serves the film's one question.** Cut the good tangent and keep it for another film. Name what was cut.
- **Make every number tangible** with a comparison.
- **No filler:** "interestingly", "fun fact", "in this video", "let's dive in".
- **Know why it ended before writing the ending** (user, 2026-11-01). Find out why the people involved acted when they did (the deal, the surrender, the handshake), put that reason in the film if it's sourced, and make the end question fit it. The Hans Island Short asked "Fair split? Or should one side have kept the whole rock?", but Canada and Denmark split it on purpose, months after Russia invaded Ukraine, to show border disputes can end peacefully (AP, Euronews): the user found the question "dumb" once the true story was known. The question became "What would your country have left on the rock?"
- **Narrator only.** No character voices (user, 2026-10-29).
- **The narrator is Kokoro `am_fenrir` at speed 1.1** (user, 2026-11-03: "much better than the old one", replacing `af_heart` at 1.08; then, on the Pheasant Island Short, at 1.0 "the voice is slow, not like normal human speaking speed, it will be awkward with the native", so 1.1). The speed is Kokoro's own setting, not a time-stretch afterwards; "never speed up the voice" (§2) means never squeezing it to fit a length. Pheasant Island at 1.1 with beats 0.45 s and holds 0.65 s: 189 wpm overall, against 171 at 1.0 with longer pauses. Voice it with `core/tts/tts.py`, one call per scene, so Kokoro's own sentence pauses carry the flow. Measured on a 60 s time-zones script: about 188 wpm, pitch range 14 semitones against `af_heart`'s 8.5 (the flattest voice tested). Its pauses are short and even (0.23–0.40 s), so put the longer beats (after the hook, the surprise, a question) in yourself between scenes. Tested and set aside, files in `films/_voice-test/`:
  - **Chatterbox** (MIT, commercial use OK; installed in `~/.lemo/chatterbox`): more intonation and real question rises, but it needs 3 takes per sentence plus a Whisper check (about 1 in 6 takes gets a word wrong), and it can't voice a whole Short in one pass on an 8 GB GPU. The builder is `films/_voice-test/tools/cb_build.py`.
  - **OmniVoice:** its model weights are CC-BY-NC, so it can't be used on the channel.
  - **The user's own recorded voice:** the most expressive of all (pitch range 18 semitones), but parked by the user.
- **On-screen text** only where the style itself would put it; captions go in the `.srt`.

## 6. Titles and thumbnails

**Short title:**
- the story with its concrete detail;
- the hook in the first ~40 characters (the Shorts shelf cuts there);
- under 60 characters;
- a question only if it's natural;
- 1–3 hashtags at the end;
- **the press's own name for the story, when it has one** ("the Whisky War", "the Pig War", "the Football War"), in the first 40 characters (user, 2026-11-01). People already know it and repeat it in comments; "this rock" or "this man" means nothing on the shelf, where no picture sits next to the words. The Hans Island title went from "Canada and Denmark fought over this rock with booze" to "The Whisky War: Canada vs Denmark over one rock". The name must not overstate: "a 49-year Whisky War" was rejected because the bottles only start in 1984.
- **the channel's niche question, when the story is about who owns a piece of land** (user, 2026-11-03): ask it the way people type it, "Why does X own Y?" / "How did X get Y?" (`research/niche-research-2026-11-03.md`: History Matters' "Why does Russia own Kaliningrad?" 6.9M; autocomplete "why does Denmark own Greenland"), with the place's real name, never "this island". The Pheasant Island title went from "Pheasant Island: France and Spain take turns" (an oddity, no niche question) to "Why do France and Spain both own Pheasant Island?". It must stay literally true (here "both own" is the 1856 treaty's "par indivis") and never give the answer.

**Short description: short** (user, 2026-11-01: "why the description is too long"). One hook line, the comment question, the Credits line the licences require, 1–3 hashtags. Never retell the film: it spoils the story and buries the question. The Hans Island description had retold every beat (gap, visits, photos, reason, split, swap) and was cut to four short blocks.

Good: "Argentina flew 20 beavers to the end of the world". Bad: "In 1765, Britain taxed THIS 10× more than…" (vague); "Africa in Ink: 54 Countries…" (art words, no hook).

**Landscape title:**
- the searchable keyword first, plus curiosity;
- never the answer;
- never the art technique ("in Watercolour", "Animated History").

Good: "Why Only 2 African Countries Escaped Colonization".

**Thumbnail (landscape, 1280×720):**
- One subject, big, with high contrast. No split panels, no collage.
- 2–4 big words that add to the title. A hook or mystery, **never the payoff** (the "ONLY 2 FREE" map labelled Liberia and Ethiopia gave the answer away).
- Text in the left or right two-thirds, clear of the duration badge.
- Passes the phone check at 320 px wide.
- **Words read one way only** (user, 2026-11-01, Greenland: "Not one shot? What does it mean?"). "NOT ONE SHOT" could mean a photo or a drink; it became "NEVER CONQUERED". Read the words cold, with no film in your head: if any word has a second meaning, change it.
- **It needs a feeling, not only a fact** (user, 2026-11-01: the first "NEVER CONQUERED" map was "quite good… but not enough"). What gave it punch: the subject with a face and an emotion (Greenland in its flag, shocked), the rest of the map dimmed so the subject and its target stand out, and one arrow plus a label to the second side (tiny Denmark). Show 2–3 variants side by side at full size and at 320 px, and let the user pick.
- Model: the Antarctica thumbnail ("NO COUNTRY CLAIMS THIS" + one arrow), with fewer words.

**The hover preview is our opening, muted** (checked 2026-11-01; [YouTube Help: Video previews](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7074781)). With inline playback (on by default), hovering a landscape video on Home plays it from 0:00 without sound, with captions on; viewers who turned that off get a 3-second clip YouTube picks itself. Creators can't choose the segment. So the first 10 s of a landscape film must make sense with no sound: the hook as clear captions (always upload the `.srt`), a moving image that matches the thumbnail, no talking-only opening over a still.

**Deliver** `thumbnail.jpg` with every landscape film, and `poster-short.jpg` (the first frame) with every Short.

YouTube category: **Education**. "Made for kids": **No**.

## 7. Facebook

- **Reels:** the Short, the same video as on YouTube. Never also the landscape cut: Meta cuts reach for duplicates, and it split our views on 2026-10-21.
- **Reel caption,** hand-written:
  - line 1, the hook;
  - line 2, one line of the main point;
  - line 3, an easy comment prompt (an opinion, or "Had you heard this?").

  No chapters, no #shorts.
- **Our own first comment,** posted right after every post: the source, or one fact the post left out, ending on a question. It can carry its own image (the Dracula post's comment: the empty grave at Snagov). We're testing this (§12).
- **Photo posts** (model: The Historian's Den, 2026-10-31):
  - **Image:** one real image of the person or object, big, with a small round inset of the strange detail. On it, a 2–4 line story in plain words that already holds the twist, with the key words in a highlight colour.
  - **Text:** line 1 a feeling ("It doesn't get much spookier than this."), then the full story in 150–250 words of short paragraphs, ending on an open question, then 1–2 hashtags.
  - Images commercially usable only; facts in `FACTS.md` like any film.

## 8. Posting

- **Testing 2 a day, from 2026-11-05 to 2026-11-15** (user, 2026-11-03: "I think the frequency doesn't matter"; started early on 2026-11-04: "We already start the 2 post within 12h so we can continue with that this week"; see Open tests, §12). Two Shorts a day, about 13 h apart, at **10:00 and 23:00 Vietnam time**. An event story and a place story each day, swapping slots daily so the time comparison isn't a topic comparison; never two stories with the same main country on one day. Each one goes out as both the YouTube Short and the Facebook Reel. Never two posts in the same hour on either platform. The bad cases below were all posts bunched within minutes or hours. 2 a day spaced 12 h apart has never been tried. Until the test is read, the rule below stays the default.
- **What the research says about a new post hurting earlier ones** (checked 2026-11-04, user: "don't base on my own data, it could be because that short has low quality"). Our own bunched cases below can't separate bunching from weak Shorts, so they aren't proof.
  - **YouTube: no evidence of harm.** YouTube's Shorts lead Todd Sherman said on Creator Insider (2023, as reported by Benzinga) that posting even 10 a day doesn't affect performance; YouTube judges "video and topic", not the channel. Rene Ritchie, YouTube's creator liaison, posted 3 Shorts a day himself in 2024. The only independent test (OverseerOS, 466 long videos on 12 channels, 2026) found older videos' daily views *rose* after a new upload (median 1.12×), but it didn't cover Shorts and the sample is small. What does fade a Short is its age: since late 2025 creators report YouTube favouring new Shorts over older ones (Tubefilter, 2025-12-05; not confirmed by YouTube).
  - **Facebook: no Meta guidance, weak outside data.** Meta publishes no per-day reach limit for a Page. The only data is on ordinary Page posts, not Reels: for brand Pages each extra post in a day reached somewhat fewer people; for media Pages the effect was "barely present" (Sotrender). Blogs claiming "Reels compete with each other" cite no data. Spacing posts a few hours apart costs nothing, so keep it.
- **YouTube:** 1 Short a day, the same one as the Facebook Reel, until the 2-a-day test is read (2026-11-01). On 2026-10-29 the Korea Short went out with two others within 7 h: Korea got 1,316 views, the other two 140 and 92 (correlation only; the other two may simply have been weaker). Metricool (2026, 799,718 videos) found views *per Short* highest at 2–4 uploads a week, while a channel's *total* monthly views kept rising with more uploads (correlation only). Quality first; posting every day matters more than bursts.
- **Facebook:** 1 Reel a day, never two in the same hour. Our own bunched cases (correlation only, see the research above):
  - 2026-10-21: five Reels in 14 h left the last one at reach 1;
  - 2026-10-23: two Reels in the same minute got 34 and 1 views;
  - 2026-10-30: two Reels 2 minutes apart both stayed small.
- **Reel time:** 23:00 or 01:00 Vietnam time (user, 2026-10-31). That's midday in the US and evening in Europe. Korea (23:22, 130,000 plays) went out then. Photo posts go at least 6 h away from the Reel. A post tied to a date goes early on that day (Halloween: 11:00–12:00).
- **Time zones:** US evening ≈ 07:00–10:00 Vietnam time; Europe evening ≈ 00:00–03:00. Prefer the Page's own Insights as they grow.
- Shorts on the same subject go out on different days.
- Never re-upload a Short that flopped; carry the lesson into the next film.

## 10. Facts

- Every claim in the voice and on screen goes into `FACTS.md` with a current source, worded as the source has it.
- **The hook must be literally true for every case it covers.** "Two wars never officially ended" was wrong for Japan–Russia (the state of war ended in 1956; only the peace treaty is missing), so it became "never got a peace treaty".
- **Check every record twice:** every "biggest / fastest / only / first", population and count. The book may be out of date (the An-225, "the world's largest plane", was destroyed in 2022). An outdated book fact can become the hook.
- If the numbers come from one chronicler, say so in the script ("a chronicler wrote").

## 11. Quality gate (before building, and again before delivery)

1. **Value:** the takeaway sentence (§0) is written in `TREATMENT.md`, and the finished film delivers it.
2. **Topic:** fits the niche (§0: who got which land, how, and why it's still that way), and passes the five checks in §0.
3. **Hook:** reading only the first line, you want to keep watching.
4. **Facts:** every claim is in `FACTS.md`.
5. **Style, before any animation:** render 2–3 key frames with the style's own engine and tile them next to `styles/<slug>/poster.jpg` at the same size. Name the style's §1 features you can see in them. If a frame looks cheaper, or reads as a headline plus an icon on an empty card, redraw it. Passing the text checks never makes up for this one.
6. **Real evidence:** every named person has a real portrait, and every moment with a usable real picture uses it.
7. **Title and thumbnail:** concrete, under the length limit, no answer, no art words; the 320 px check passes.
8. **Ending:** the last beat pays off the hook.
9. **Variety:** the subject, the hook formula and the style differ from the last few films.
10. **Relevance:** every fact serves the one question; name what was cut.
11. **Sound:** the score is chosen per scene and sits under the voice; every effect is tied to something visible; no drawing sounds. No wooden ship creaks under boats (user, 2026-11-01: "annoying"); waves, gulls or oars carry a ship scene. **Effects are recorded sounds from the channel library `E:\lemo-sfx`**, in Shorts and long films alike (user, 2026-11-03):
   - **Not synthesized:** no `core/audio/sfx.py` sweeps, whooshes, pops or dings. The sound study (`research/sound-study/FINDINGS-2026-11-03.md`) found our synthesized arrow and wipe sweeps read as "flies, zippers, insects", while a 1.8M-view long film used recorded sounds of the story's objects (coins and clinks for a story about silver).
   - **Concrete over generic:** prefer the story's own objects (coins for money, ice for Greenland, paper and a stamp for a treaty, a cannon for a battle) to a neutral UI sound.
   - **How to find one:** search the library by what the sound really is: `~/.lemo/chatterbox/Scripts/python.exe E:/lemo-sfx/tools/index.py --find whoosh --max 1.5`. Check the top tags match the need; the index flags misnamed files, such as mouth sounds filed as "pops".
   - **When the library lacks something:** add it to the library (Freesound CC0 via `E:/lemo-sfx/tools/freesound_kit.py`, or a Sonniss or Kenney pack), index it, then use it. Never fetch effects into a single film's folder.
   - **Log it:** every effect used goes into the film's `MATERIAL.md` with its library path and source (`freesound/catalog.json` has the author and URL). Sources and licences are in `E:\lemo-sfx\README.md`.

   **Background music** (user, 2026-11-03: "the tempo is a little too slow"; measured in `research/sound-study/FINDINGS-2026-11-03.md` against a 1.8M-view long film):
   - **Tempo: about 110–130 BPM, not 96.** Our scores sat at a fixed 96 BPM with 2.7 notes a second. The reference averaged 115 BPM (96–136) with 4.0 notes a second. Go below 110 only for a reflective beat (a death, the open question at the end).
   - **Shorts: one theme, one palette, one tempo** for the whole Short (user, 2026-10-28: instrument changes every scene felt jumpy). Build and thin it by layers (bass, ostinato, percussion), keep the drop before the reveal, and keep comic stings rare.
   - **The palette follows the story's tone, and changes from film to film** (user, 2026-11-03, Pheasant Island: the pizzicato + marimba + glockenspiel theme in a major key "sounds like the comedy or for fun fact"; the remake in strings and horn was "better"). Pick one palette per film from the list below, and write the choice and why in `TREATMENT.md`. Within a palette, every film gets its own key, chord loop, tempo (110–130) and melody, so no two consecutive Shorts sound alike (the same variety check as §11.9).
     - **Documentary** (history told straight: wars, treaties, kings, a handover): a cello/viola spiccato pulse, sustained strings over a double bass, a French-horn theme in long notes; timpani for conflict, harp alone for a tender moment. Minor key. *Pheasant Island, 2026-11-03.*
     - **Tension** (a standoff, a spy story, a disaster, the Cold War): low strings tremolo, a muted-trumpet or low-brass motif, a staccato bass pulse, timpani; minor, sparse.
     - **Wonder** (exploration, a strange place, nature, the ice, a discovery): harp or piano arpeggios, sustained strings, a flute or clarinet melody, soft bells; major or modal, open chords.
     - **Playful** (a petty rivalry with a wink: bottles on a rock, a flagpole race, a whisky war): pizzicato strings and marimba over a pizzicato bass. Only for these; never for a war, a death or a treaty.
     - **Regional colour, as one layer:** one instrument that hints at the place (guitar for Spain or Latin America, a pipa or guqin for China, a koto for Japan) over one of the palettes above, never a pastiche of a real tune.
     All instruments come from the sampler's CC0 libraries (`core/audio/INSTRUMENTS.md`).
   - **Long films: change the palette at chapter breaks.** One theme can run through the film, but each chapter (or a big turn) gets its own instrument colour and tempo: strings, then piano, then flute and percussion, and faster for drama (the reference ran 129–136 BPM in its most dramatic chapters). Within a chapter the Shorts rule holds: layers, not new instruments. Our Greenland film kept violins in 88% of its 16 minutes at one tempo, and its music changes didn't register as changes.
   - **Levels: about 4 dB lower than before** (user, 2026-11-04, the Toledo long film: "the background is a little loud, it distracts me from the main voice"). Measured on that mix (stems, median over speech): music about 24 dB under the voice while it speaks (was about 16–17), rising about 5 dB in the voice's gaps (mixer: music bus 0.36, duck 0.58; effects bus 0.42, duck 0.75). Measure the stems (`STEMS=1 tools/mix.py`), don't estimate. Cut it for a beat before the key number or the reveal.
   - **Narration pace holds through the whole film,** about 175–180 wpm. Greenland slowed from about 178 to 148–157 wpm in its second half, where a long film needs to lift.
12. **No clipped text:** extract a full-size frame at the midpoint of every subtitle cue and every piece of text the style draws. Every line wraps inside its box, at least 60 px from the frame edges. The library's text check doesn't catch overflow (missed in the Antarctica remake, 2026-10-23).

Then the library's own checks (DIRECTOR.md §11).

## 12. Reading the results

- **YouTube Short:** Studio → "Viewed vs. swiped away". About 70%+ is good. If it's low, the hook or first frame failed. If it's good but views stall, the middle drags (check the retention graph).
- **Don't judge a Short on its first day.** YouTube tests it on a small audience, then widens in waves. The Korea Short only started growing after about 15 h; Camp Century (posted 2026-11-02 23:00 VN) sat flat for about 1 day 10 h before views came (user, 2026-11-04).
  - Early on, read the swipe rate and the retention curve, not views.
  - Call it at about 48 h, never before: a Short can stay flat for most of that time and still take off.
  - It's a miss only if it's still flat after about 3 days *and* the swipe rate is bad.
  - Good swipe rate but few views means not pushed yet: give it a week.

  The Analytics API lacks viewed vs. swiped away; read that in Studio.
- **Read each Short's country mix** (2026-11-04): its US + Europe views and watch time, not only the total. A Short that wins mostly in one far-off country (the warship in the Philippines, Wagah in Indonesia) is not evidence for a long film (§0, "Who has to care"). The API (`dimensions=country`, `filters=video==ID`) hides countries with very few views, and the last 2–3 days aren't in yet.
- **Facebook Reel:** plays, average watch time and the retention graph. The Page token reads our own Reels (`/{page_id}/video_reels`, then `video_insights` per video, including `post_video_retention_graph`). Our baseline on 2026-10-31:

- **Landscape:** Studio → Reach. Under about 100 impressions, YouTube barely showed it. No CTR number is a pass mark on its own (YouTube Help, checked 2026-11-01; the old "under 2–3% = bad packaging" rule was too blunt):
  - YouTube's only benchmark: half of all channels and videos sit between 2% and 10%. Videos under a week old or under 100 views vary even more.
  - **Read CTR per traffic source** (Reach → impressions by traffic source), never the blended number. Search gets few impressions and a high CTR; Home/Browse gets many impressions and a low CTR. A video mostly shown on Home will look low next to one shown in Search.
  - **CTR falls as reach widens.** YouTube's own example: 9% at 10,000 impressions became 3.5% at 100,000, "a sign of success". Early CTR is inflated by loyal viewers. A low CTR *with* growing impressions is not a failure.
  - **Compare with our own videos** from the same source, not with internet averages. Third-party "good CTR" tables disagree with each other and aren't evidence.
  - Packaging is the likely problem only when impressions stall *and* CTR is below our own videos from the same source. Then check the average view duration too: a click that leaves fast doesn't help.
  - Test packaging with Studio's **Test & Compare** (up to 3 thumbnails, about 2 weeks). It picks the winner by **watch time share, not CTR**, so it also rejects clickbait.
  - Impressions leave out external sites and end screens, so views from Facebook or links don't show in CTR.
- **One test variable per film,** written in the film's `YOUTUBE.md` before publishing, with the 48 h results next to it. Change one thing at a time (DIRECTING-PLAYBOOK §6).

## Evidence behind these rules

- **Strong:**
  - The hook in the first 1–3 s (YouTube's own "viewed vs. swiped away" metric).
  - Guessing before an answer improves memory of that answer (St. Hilaire et al. 2024 meta-analysis: g = 0.65 with a guess, 0.22 without). We still don't use a spoken guess prompt: the user found it hurts delivery (2026-10-31).
  - Stories beat plain explanation for understanding (meta-analysis, 33,000 people).
  - Off-topic "seductive details" lower learning (two meta-analyses).
  - Guiding the eye helps (103 studies).
  - Shorter videos and more enthusiastic speakers hold people longer (Guo et al. 2014, 6.9M edX sessions).

  Details: `DIRECTING-PLAYBOOK.md` and `research/directing/`.
- **Moderate:**
  - Too-vague question headlines lose clicks (about 9,000 headline tests).
  - Long Shorts win only with high view duration (Galloway, 5,400 Shorts).
  - Captions help comprehension (Gernsbacher 2015).
  - Replays count as views since 2025-03-31.
  - YouTube's and Meta's duplicate-content rules (2025).
  - Music changes how the same images are read (lab studies).
  - Views per Short peak at 2–4 uploads a week (Metricool 2026, correlation).
- **What big Shorts audiences do** (2026-10-31, 11,550 comments): `research/audience-questions-2026-10-31.md`.
- **No evidence either way:** US vs European taste in tone or pacing; what makes educational Shorts get finished; the right gap between question and answer. Our own analytics decide these.
- **Judgment:** posting times and gaps, re-hook at ~20 s, a visual change every 2–3 s, thumbnail word count.
- **Our own data:**
  - Famous subject + surprising fact won: Korea flagpole (YouTube 1,265 views, Facebook 130,000+ plays), Caspian, Everest, Africa 1914.
  - Page facts about little-known subjects stayed at a few hundred.
  - Dense or vague openings got under 20–90 views (2026-10-21/22).
  - Two Reels in the same hour killed the second.
  - Two sides acting against each other beat single-place oddities and lone people, week of 26 Oct (§0, §12 table).
  - Three Shorts on one day (2026-10-29): the strongest took nearly all the views.
