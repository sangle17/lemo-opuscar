# Directing playbook: the director's questions, built on evidence

A companion to `CHANNEL-RULES.md` (what the channel requires) and `LONG-FORM.md` (how long films are made). This file says **how to think** while making any film for the channel: the questions a director asks at each stage, and the evidence behind them. Agreed with the user on 2026-10-27 after a research round; the sources, methods, limits and spot-checks are in `films/research/directing/` (local only: `1-audience.md`, `2-psychology.md`, `3-craft.md`, `4-who-is-interested.md`).

**The idea in one line:** ask the viewer a real question, answer it with a story, and make every second serve that one question.

Grades as in the research: **Strong** (meta-analyses, replicated), **Moderate** (one good study or large survey), **Weak** (small study, other content type), **Craft** (what masters do, untested). Most evidence measured memory and understanding in labs or MOOCs, **not** whether people keep watching a Short. Where it matters, that is said.

## 1. Before writing: who is this for, and what is the question?

- **Who.** Interest in history is broad: only 8% of US adults have none (AHA 2021, Moderate). They come to be informed and, almost as much, to be entertained. Documentaries are their main source of history in the US and UK, so a documentary is the bar we're measured against, not another Short.
- **The topic picks the audience.** War, empire and Rome lean strongly male; human stories (Salem, the Titanic, the Tudors) draw men and women about equally (YouGov US 2023, UK 2024, Moderate). Younger Americans lean towards foreign places and peoples, older ones towards their own country (AHA 2021). Choose knowingly.
- **The question.** Write the one question the film answers before anything else. If you can't, there is no film yet (`LONG-FORM.md` §1, the spine test, holds for Shorts too).

## 2. Writing: curiosity, story, relevance

**2.1 Open a real question, then answer it (Strong for memory).** People remember answers they were curious about (Kang 2009, Gruber 2014). Every extra wait lowers willingness to wait, so don't sit on one question for the whole film: **answer it, then open the next one**. A chain of small questions beats one long tease. No study tells us the right gap for a Short; read it from our retention graphs (§6).

**2.2 Let them guess (Strong for memory).** Asking a question before the material makes its answer stick, and asking people to *guess* roughly triples the effect (prequestion meta-analysis, St. Hilaire et al. 2024: g = 0.65 with a guess vs 0.22 without). It helps only the answer to that question, not the rest of the film (g = 0.04). So:
- A **guess beat** fits any shape, not only the quiz: one spoken line ("Where would you put it?") and a pause over a moving picture. The quiz stays one option among four (CHANNEL-RULES §1).
- Point the guess at the one fact you most want remembered.
- Untested: whether a silent, unspoken guess gives the same benefit as a written one.

**2.3 Tell it as things happening (Strong for understanding, Moderate for memory).** Stories beat plain explanation: comprehension g = .48, memory g = .72 (Mar et al. 2021, 33,000 people; the memory figure is inflated by publication bias). Viewers sense a new event when an **action or goal changes**, not when the place or the cut changes, and they remember best what is on screen at that moment (Zacks; Magliano & Zacks 2011). So:
- Write the film as a chain of actions: someone decides, sails, signs, flies 20 beavers south. Facts hang on the actions.
- **Put the key number or the reveal at a turn**, where an action changes.
- A person on screen helps because it creates action and stakes. Don't rely on "one named victim" magic: the one direct replication of that study failed.

**2.4 Every detail serves the question (Strong).** Interesting but off-topic details lower learning ("seductive details", two meta-analyses). Strong curiosity can even make unrelated facts shown nearby *worse* remembered (Keller et al. 2024). So a fun fact earns its place only if it **is** the main point or leads straight into it; cut the good tangent and keep it for another film. This is why "main point + fun fact" grows the fact out of the subject (CHANNEL-RULES §1).

**2.5 Emotion: wonder and human stakes, not alarm (Weak–Moderate, other content).** High-arousal emotions, awe among them, predict sharing (Berger & Milkman 2012). On 22,743 news-headline tests, sad words raised clicks while fear and joy words lowered them (Robertson et al. 2023). Those measure news, not history films, so treat this as a direction to test: hooks built on wonder ("the world is stranger than the map") and on people's stakes, never fear-bait.

## 3. Staging: one thing at a time

**The story sets the scenes; the picture follows the voice** (user, 2026-10-27). A new scene starts when the story moves to a new point or action, never one picture per sentence and never a fixed count. Inside a scene the camera goes to whatever the voice is talking about right now, the way Ken Burns explores one photograph, and nothing else on screen competes with it. When eye and ear disagree, the eye wins: viewers remembered narration better when the pictures matched it, and lost the words when they didn't (Drew & Grimes 1987, TV news, Moderate). The board or map is home base for **where** (orientation, the journey between points); full frame is for **what** and **proof** (a document, a photo, one close-up), big enough to read on a phone. Full-frame material is also lighter to render than a busy board, which leaves the effort for the story.

**Order of work:** (1) story first: the question and the chain of actions (§2); (2) a beat list from the script: for each point, what the viewer must *see* to understand or believe it; (3) only then the visuals.

- **Guide the eye (Strong).** Pointing at what matters helps (signalling meta-analysis, 103 studies, g = 0.53); flashing or blinking cues hurt. Our styles' own moves are the pointers: the loupe, the roundel, the ink pull, the stamped route. Use them to say "look here", never as decoration, and never one competing with another.
- **Show change, not decoration (Strong).** Animation helps when it shows the subject changing (a border moving, ice retreating, a route growing), not when it only decorates (Höffler & Leutner 2007, d = 0.37).
- **Viewers look where everyone looks (Moderate).** In edited film, viewers' eyes and brains stay in step far more than with unedited footage (Smith 2012; Hasson 2008). Stage so the next subject appears where the eye already is.
- The look itself belongs to the style (CHANNEL-RULES §2).

## 4. Sound: it gives the picture its meaning

- **Music changes what viewers think they saw (Moderate).** The same images read differently, and are remembered differently a week later, under different scores (Boltz 2001). Choose the score for the feeling the scene needs, scene by scene, not one bed for the whole film.
- **Music shouldn't compete with the voice (Moderate, measured on learning).** Background music and arbitrary sounds reduced learning; a few sounds each tied to one on-screen event did not (Moreno & Mayer 2000). So: music under the voice, every effect tied to something visible, no beds of arbitrary noise (and no drawing sounds, per the user). These studies measured learning, not watch time.
- **Silence is a tool (Craft).** Cut the music for a beat before the key number or the reveal.

## 5. The voice and the cut

- **Energy, not speed (Moderate).** In 6.9M edX sessions faster speakers held people longer, and the authors read speaking rate as a sign of enthusiasm (Guo et al. 2014; untested there). Experiments since: enthusiasm raises the wish to watch the next video, not quiz scores. Direct the voice for energy and warmth; never speed it up to fit (memory: short length flexible).
- **Pauses stay (no contrary evidence).** Guo's "edit out pauses" was edX producers' habit about lecture filler, not a measured result. Designed pauses (CHANNEL-RULES §5) were never tested against.
- **Synthetic voice:** modern neural voices are no worse for learning, but voice quality changes how much viewers trust the narrator (Moderate). Use the best voice available.
- **Cut on movement or sound (Moderate + Craft).** Cuts feel invisible on a motion onset, a gaze shift or a sound (Smith 2012; Murch's eye-trace). Murch's blink theory is a metaphor, not the mechanism.
- **Shape of a long film (Moderate, from 150 Hollywood features).** Slower setup, fastest cutting early in the climax, a quiet coda (Cutting 2016). A Short has no slow setup: the hook comes first (CHANNEL-RULES §2).

## 6. Every film is an experiment

What the research **can't** tell us: US vs European taste in tone, humour or pacing (no real studies); what makes an educational Short get finished or rewatched (nothing peer-reviewed); the right gap between question and answer; vertical video at all. Our own analytics are the only evidence there. So:

- **One test variable per film**, chosen before publishing and written in the film's `YOUTUBE.md`, e.g. "guess beat at 0:12 vs none", "awe hook vs number hook", "score drops out before the reveal".
- After about 48 h, write down "viewed vs swiped away", average view duration and the retention graph's shape, next to the variable.
- Change one thing at a time; after about 10 films, compare and turn what holds up into a rule here.

## 6b. Rules for the script debate

An audit of 11 films (2026-10-27) found the two-agent debate itself causing two faults. From now on both reviewers apply these:
- **A re-hook must serve the question.** The hook/retention reviewer kept vivid side facts "to hold attention" (Velázquez's death, "half the world's oil", Esperanto, Marco Polo's camels). Those are seductive details (2.4): cut them, or find a re-hook inside the story.
- **Fix a question line, don't cut it.** "So whose is he?", "Why go to all that trouble?" and "How?" were cut for length or wording; they were the curiosity gaps and the natural guess beats (2.1, 2.2). Reword until it's true; keep the question.
- **No numbers after the answer.** Late figures (51/111 enclaves, 1998/2006, 1991/2003) do no work once the question is answered.
- **Explain-shaped scripts get rebuilt as actions.** If the middle reads "what it is / how it works / why", find who did what, and put the key fact at that turn (2.3).

## 7. The director's checklist

Before writing:
1. What is the one question? Who is it for (§1)?
2. What will the viewer look at? Where a map helps (places, distances, borders), use it; where it doesn't, plan scenes, real pictures or portraits instead. The map is a tool, not a requirement.
3. Who are the named people, and is there a real portrait of each, and a real period picture of the key moments, that the holder allows for commercial use? Search before scripting (LONG-FORM §2b).

Script:
4. Does the opening stop on the question, without summarising the answer? (Teasers, never the payoff.)
5. Is it a chain of actions, with the key fact at a turn (2.3)?
6. Where is the guess beat, and which fact is it for (2.2)?
7. Does every fact serve the question? What did we cut (2.4)?
8. Which feeling does each scene need: wonder, stakes, poignancy (2.5)?
9. Narrator only: no character voices, no lines in a country's mouth (user, 2026-10-29).

Staging and sound:
10. In each shot, what is the one thing to look at, and what points to it (§3)?
11. Is every movement showing change, not decoration (§3)? Is the stage cleared before every camera move, so no text slides with the map?
12. Which lines carry a number or key fact worth putting on screen? Only those; never a weak phrase.
13. Where a line says something happens, is it shown as action, a cutaway scene or a real picture, rather than a label? Not every line, only where it helps.
14. Does every named person get a real portrait when first named? Does every moment with a real period picture use it instead of a drawing?
15. Does any scene tell two things on one background, or pile several things on one spot? Split it.
16. Does every face, card and ring have a job the voice explains? Cut the rest.
17. Which score for which scene; where does it drop out; is every sound tied to something visible (§4)? Real actions get recorded CC0 effects (musket, hooves, door, stamp).

Before publishing:
18. Is every picture, sound and font checked for commercial use on the holder's own record, and logged in `MATERIAL.md`?
19. Text checks pass (reading time, edges), and the user has listened to the sound (the agent can't hear it).
20. What is this film's test variable (§6)?
