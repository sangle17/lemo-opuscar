# Long-form films (12–20 min): how to make one from scratch

A companion to `CHANNEL-RULES.md`. Those rules cover Shorts and the 2:30–3:00 landscape cut; this file covers the long film, made without an earlier short film to borrow from. First written 2026-10-23, from planning *The Promise* (`films/the-promise/`).

**The main lesson:** a long film costs the same as a short one at every stage except animation. So get the cheap stages completely right (spine, facts, script, voice) before drawing anything. Then build a small kit of scene types once and assemble every chapter from it.

---

## 1. Should this topic be a long film? (the spine test)

Make a long film only if all three are true:

1. **One question carries the whole film.** For example: "Who was 'all men' really for?" or "Why did only two African countries stay free?" If the honest description is "facts about Asia", it's a quiz-round film or Shorts only (CHANNEL-RULES §4).
2. **The chapters are linked by cause, not by topic.** Each chapter's ending raises the next chapter's question ("…and in Africa, a storm was coming"). If you could shuffle the chapters, there's no spine.
3. **There are at least 8 wow facts** (CHANNEL-RULES §2 wow test), about one every 90 seconds. Fewer means the film will feel like a lecture.

**Picking the topic (learned from "How Mexico lost half its land in 30 years", 2026-10-28/29).** These helped; they are pluses, not requirements:
- **Something to show**: a map that changes (land changing hands, a route) or strong real pictures and scenes. The map is one tool among several, not a must.
- **A title that is a question with a number**, searchable, never the answer ("How Mexico Lost Half Its Land in 30 Years").
- **A hook the audience lives in**: today's places (Los Angeles, Las Vegas, Phoenix) on land the story is about.
- **Named people and real pictures**: a cast of 5–10 people with real portraits and period prints of the key moments (LoC "No known restrictions", NARA "Unrestricted", Smithsonian CC0). Check this in the material scout before scripting; a topic with no real pictures leans entirely on drawn scenes.
- **A twist at about 60 %** (here: the gold found nine days before the treaty) and a refrain the ending can pay off ("on paper").
- **Several Shorts inside it** that stand alone (each with its own hook), noted in the hook bank.

**Raw material needed:** about 4–6 book pages on related subjects, plus research. A page gives about 15–25 usable facts; a 15-minute film uses 60–80. The facts that **connect** the pages are rarely in the book: they come from research (in *The Promise*: Banneker's letter, Liberia's founding, King in Ghana, Du Bois's death). Budget for that.

## 2. The order of work (cheapest first, with stop points)

| Step | Output | Time | Stop point |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Spine | One question, its answer, 3 open loops, a one-line POV | 20–30 min | Show the user the question and the 3 loops. |
| 2. Fact bank | `FACTS.md`, wow facts tagged **[HOOK]** | 60–90 min | Fewer than 8 hooks → it becomes Shorts or a shorter film. |
| 2b. Material scout | A quick search for real clips and images, per chapter (§5b) | 30–45 min | A chapter with nothing real and nothing drawable in the kit → rethink it before scripting. |
| 3. Beat sheet | One row per chapter: re-hook, 3–5 facts, forward question, **word budget** | 20 min | The word budgets add up to the target length (see §4). |
| 4b. Material log | `MATERIAL.md`: every beat marked clip / image / drawn, with source, licence and what it really shows (§5b) | 60–90 min | Every clip and image is licence-checked before the animatic. |
| 4. Script v1 | `SCRIPT.md`, every line tagged with a fact ID, written for the ear (§4b) | 45–60 min | **Count the words with a script; don't estimate.** (*The Promise* v1 was planned at 15 min and counted at 9–10.) Then the read-aloud test: no numbered signposts, no textbook phrasing. |
| 5. Voice draft | Full TTS read of the script, plus speech check | 15–30 min | Listen once at 1×. Cut anything you'd skip. Voice tools: `core/README.md`. |
| 6. Animatic | Still frames on the voice track, timed per line | 30–45 min | The pacing works with stills only. If it drags here, animation won't save it. |
| 7. Kit | One style; 6–10 reusable scene types (§5) built and checked against `STYLE.md` | 1.5–2.5 h | This is the expensive part, and it's done once. |
| 8. Chapters | One chapter per session, rendered on its own | 45–90 min each | Each chapter is checked before the next one starts. |
| 9. Assembly | Concatenate, mix, captions, chapters, thumbnail, Shorts | 1–1.5 h | Quality gate (CHANNEL-RULES §11) plus DIRECTOR.md §11. |

**Total for 15 min:** about 10–14 hours, spread over 5–8 sessions. Steps 1–6 (about 4 hours) produce a script, a voice track and an animatic. Those can be judged, and dropped, before any expensive work starts.

## 3. Structure template (15 min)

| Time | Part | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:25 | **Hook** | The contradiction or question in the first 10 s, over a moving image of the subject. Same rules as a Short's first frame. |
| 0:25–0:55 | **Pre-hook (the promise)** | The spine question, then three teasers phrased so they don't give their outcome away ("why Mexico invited thousands of Americans in, on purpose"). These are the open loops. **Never summarise the answer here** (see §4c). |
| 0:55–~8:00 | **Chapters 1–3** | Setup and scale, then the story builds. Each chapter is 1:45–2:30. |
| ~8:00–9:30 | **Midpoint twist** | At 55–65% of the film, the story turns ("So Liberia is the hero. Right? Not quite."). This is where long videos lose people, so the strongest surprise goes here. |
| ~9:30–14:00 | **Chapters 4–6** | The deeper "why", the human moments, the open loops paying off one by one. |
| last 60 s | **Ending** | Return to the opening image, give the answer to the spine question in one line, a POV line, a comment question, a teaser for the next video. |

**Each chapter is a mini-Short:** a re-hook (a concrete detail in the first sentence, never "Now let's look at…") → 3–5 facts that build → one payoff → a **forward question** that opens the next chapter. A chapter never ends on a summary.

## 4. Word budget

- The narrator (Kokoro `af_heart`) reads about **145 words a minute**, including short holds.
- 12 min ≈ 1,700 words · 15 min ≈ 2,100 · 20 min ≈ 2,800.
- Per chapter: about 250–350 words. The hook plus pre-hook is about 100.
- **If the script comes out short, add a chapter where the story has a real gap**, never pad. In *The Promise* the gap was 1865–1954, when freedom came and was taken back. "Content decides length" (CHANNEL-RULES §3) holds for long films too.

## 4b. Write for the ear, not the page

The narration is heard, not read. Write it the way a native English speaker would tell the story to a friend (CHANNEL-RULES §5; user feedback, 2026-10-23: "use the speaking language, not the reading").

- **No numbered signposts in the voice:** never "Clue one", "Fact number three", "Chapter two: …", "Firstly / Secondly", "In this section we will…". Chapter titles live in the description (and on screen only where the style draws titles); the voice moves on naturally: "Here's the thing.", "Now it gets weird.", "So what happened next?", "But here's what nobody tells you.", "Fast-forward forty years."
- **Spoken grammar:** contractions (it's, didn't, they'd); short sentences; questions to the viewer; "you" and "we". No textbook connectors ("furthermore", "moreover", "thus", "in addition", "it is worth noting", "notably").
- **Numbers as people say them:** "about four thousand", "nearly half", "twice the size of Texas", not "approximately 4,012" or "47.3%". Exact figures go in `FACTS.md` and the description; on screen only in the style's own form (a measured note, a dial, a chart the style draws).
- **No dates stacked in a row:** one date per sentence at most, and lead with the event, not the date ("Ten years later, the money ran out", not "In 1857, following the 1847 declaration, …").
- **Names once, then a handle:** "Benjamin Banneker, a self-taught astronomer" the first time, then "Banneker" or "he".
- **Pauses are written into the script, not left to chance** (user feedback, 2026-10-23: "pausing is an art"). Mark them as `[BEAT]` (~0.6 s) or `[SILENCE]` (1–2 s) and time the voice to them: a beat after every question to the viewer, after a surprising number, and at the end of each chapter's forward question; a real silence before each big reveal and before the midpoint twist. Normal sentence breaks are 0.2–0.3 s. The picture keeps moving through every pause; music or sound carries it. Budget about 10% of the running time for pauses when counting words (§4).
- **Read-aloud test at step 4 and step 5 (§2):** read every line out loud. If you'd never say it to a friend, or you run out of breath, rewrite it. In the voice draft, mark every line that sounds like it's being read off a page and fix it before the animatic.

## 4c. Build it like an essay, say it like a teacher (user, 2026-10-25)

User feedback on the first Everest and Caspian scripts: "lack of coherence… the main idea should be developed like an essay… then use public speaking techniques to make it a real speech… explain and use common words so it's not dry and academic. The goal is educating, like a teacher." A long film is a lesson with one big idea, not a string of surprising facts. Write it in three passes.

**Pass 1, the argument (essay craft).**
- **One thesis, in one plain sentence,** that answers the spine question. The viewer should be able to repeat it to a friend after watching. Everything in the film either supports it, explains it or tests it. A fact that does neither goes to a Short, however good it is (Mayer's *coherence principle*: people learn more when extra material is left out).
- **Question first, answer later (user, 2026-10-28):** Situation (what everyone knows) → Complication (the surprising problem) → Question (the spine), all in the first minute; **the answer (the thesis) is not said there.** An opening that summarised the whole answer ("a frontier it couldn't fill… a revolt, a war and a sale", *How Mexico lost half its land*, v2) gave viewers no reason to stay. The thesis is built up chapter by chapter, said for the first time near the midpoint, and in full at the end. (This replaces the earlier "answer first, Minto's SCQA" advice.)
- **Two to four parts,** each one reason for the thesis. Say the map once at the start in words, not numbers ("its name, its height, and who got there first"); this is Ausubel's *advance organizer*: the big picture first, so every later detail has a place to go.
- **Each chapter is an essay paragraph (PEEL):** **Point** (the opening line states this chapter's claim, in plain words) → **Evidence** (the story, the people, the facts) → **Explain** (what it means: "In other words…", "So what does that tell us?") → **Link** (back to the thesis, then into the next part). The *Explain* step is the one the first scripts skipped; it is where the teaching happens, and it's usually the longest.
- **Known → new:** each chapter opens from something the viewer already has ("So that's the name. But how did they know it was the highest?"), then adds one new thing.
- **Callbacks tie the parts together:** a person, an object or a phrase from an earlier part returns later with a new meaning (Odell's fossils in the height part, Odell's sighting in the "who was first" part).
- **Time order is a tool, not a rule.** Group by idea, not by date, when that teaches better; then keep dates inside each part in order.

**Pass 2, the explanation (teaching craft).**
- **Beat the curse of knowledge:** assume the viewer is a curious 14-year-old who's never heard of the subject. Every technical word is said plainly first, then named: "the air bends light. Scientists call it refraction." Or just keep the plain words.
- **One analogy for every abstract or huge idea,** from everyday life: "like measuring someone's height with or without their hat" (rock vs snow), "the way a rug bunches up when you push it against a wall" (mountains rising), "like a boat rising when you unload it". Analogies go in `FACTS.md` only when they carry a number.
- **Concrete before abstract:** the example first, then the rule.
- **Check-understanding lines:** "In other words…", "Think of it like this…", "So here's what that means."
- **Mini-recap at the end of each part:** one sentence that restates what we've learned and how it answers the question.

**Pass 3, the speech (public speaking craft).**
- **Conversational, not formal** (Mayer's *personalization principle*: people learn more from conversational narration): "you", "we", "I think"; contractions; questions to the viewer with a beat after them.
- **Listeners can't rewind:** repeat the spine question at the start, and the thesis at the midpoint and at the end (never as a summary in the opening), and give the film a **refrain**, one short phrase that returns three times ("a mountain that won't hold still"; "one word").
- **Rhythm:** groups of three; short sentences with a longer one now and then; no list longer than three items.
- **Signpost in words, never in numbers** (§4b still applies: no "first reason / second reason"): "So that's the name. Now the harder question…", "Here's where it gets strange."
- **The ending is a summary a teacher would give:** the question again, the answer in one sentence with its reasons, then an opinion, then the comment prompt.

## 5. The kit: how a long film stays affordable

The 3-minute films were expensive because almost every shot was custom-made. A long film can't work like that. Pick **one style**, copy its demo engine, and build a small set of reusable **scenes** in it that take content as data: a map drawn the style's way, a place or set the camera can travel through, a character rig, a few props, the style's own way of showing a number or a document. These are scenes, not text cards: no number cards, quote cards, title cards or case cards on flat grounds (they turned the short films into slides, 2026-10-24). Text appears only where `STYLE.md` draws it.

Then each chapter is mostly data: which scene, which place or character, which camera move, how long. New custom shots are only for the hook, the midpoint twist and the ending (3–5 in all). Check the kit against the demo poster before building chapters on it (CHANNEL-RULES §11, style test).

## 5b. Hybrid: drawn scenes, real clips and real images (the default for 15–20 min, user, 2026-10-24)

A long film mixes three kinds of material under one look. Draw only what the real material can't show.

| Material | Its job | Typical share |
|---|---|---|
| **Real clip** (3–8 s, narrated over) | Proof that it happened; the place today; scale (drone, ship, crowd) | 20–30% |
| **Real image / document** (with a slow move, crop or parallax) | Faces, maps, letters, the primary document that becomes a hook | 30–40% |
| **Drawn scene** (the style's engine) | What no camera saw: before photography, decisions, maps that move, how and why | 30–40% |

The share follows the era: before ~1850 mostly drawn plus paintings, engravings and old maps; 1890–1930 real film exists; after 1931 news film is usually paid, so images, US-government material and drawing carry it.

- **Story first, then material.** Write the spine and beats, then search per beat. A great find may earn a new line or a new hook (a named person on a document beat every book fact so far); never bend the story to fit a clip.
- **Grammar of the mix:** real for proof, drawn for explanation. Switch at a beat, not mid-sentence. A strong move: a drawn reconstruction, then "and this is the real one" with the photo.
- **One look over everything:** the same grade or print pass (e.g. the Silent Film silver, flicker and scratches) on clips, photos and drawn scenes, the same frame and type, so it reads as one film.
- **Honesty:** never present a clip from another event, place or year as the real one; a reconstruction is drawn, not passed off as real. Each item's true date and subject goes in `MATERIAL.md`.
- **Sources:** public domain before ~1931 (archive.org, Wikimedia Commons, Library of Congress), US government (NARA, NASA, military), Pexels/Pixabay for today's landscapes. Never paid news archives (Pathé, AP, Getty) or clips ripped from YouTube. Copyright differs by country: a film can be public domain in the US and not in the UK; log which.
- **Reused-content rule:** short clips with our own narration and edit; never a long uncut public-domain clip as filler.
- **Cost:** research grows by ~1.5–3 h, rendering shrinks (clips play, images move). The drawn kit (§5) covers only the beats with no real material, plus the hook, the midpoint twist and the ending.

## 5c. Scenes, not lines (user, 2026-10-25)

User feedback on the first scripts: "you split the resource too small. Two sentences for a clip? How many seconds could it last, how can the user see it?" A new picture for every sentence gives 4–8 s per image: too short to read a document, look at a face or take in a place, and it feels like flipping slides.

- **The unit is a scene:** one picture or one continuous sequence that carries one idea, under several sentences of narration. Change the picture when the idea changes, not when the sentence ends (Mayer's *signaling* and *coherence* principles).
- **Lengths:** a scene runs about **20–60 s**. Inside it the picture keeps moving: a slow push or pan across a photo, details revealed one by one, a drawn scene that builds or changes, two or three clips of the same place. A still photo holds at least 6–10 s; a document or map that viewers must read holds long enough to read it (about 1 s per 3 words, plus 2 s). A real clip runs 5–15 s; if the narration is longer, use more clips of the same place or move to a still of the same subject.
- **Budget:** a 15-minute film has about **25–40 scenes**, not 100+ lines with their own pictures. Fewer, richer scenes also cut the drawing cost (§5).
- **Script format:** `### [kind MATERIAL] what we see` opens a scene; the narration lines under it play over that picture. The Long Films page shows each scene's length and flags any under 12 s.

**Reusing old films:** reuse the art and scenes only when the long film uses the same style or tells the change of style as part of the story. Always write new narration; never stitch old cuts together.

## 6. Retention devices (glossary)

- **Hook:** a concrete contradiction or question in the first 10 s.
- **Pre-hook / promise:** the spine question plus three teasers in the first minute, none of them giving its answer away.
- **Open loop:** a question raised now and answered later. Keep 1–3 open at any time, carried by the voice and the pictures.
- **Re-hook:** the first line of each chapter, with a fresh concrete detail.
- **Forward question:** the last line of each chapter, pulling into the next.
- **Midpoint twist:** the strongest surprise, at 55–65% of the film.
- **Visual change every 2–3 s**, and a new scene type at every chapter start.
- **POV:** 1–3 opinion lines ("What gets me is…"), clearly opinion.
- **Callback ending:** return to the opening image with a new meaning.

## 7. What makes it cost more (avoid)

- Animating before the script is counted and the voice is heard.
- A new style or a new engine per chapter.
- Re-rendering the whole film for a fix in one chapter: render per chapter.
- Rendering with too many workers on texture-heavy frames (memory). Use 2.
- Research spread across production. Close every open item in `FACTS.md` before recording the voice.

## 8. After publishing

- YouTube Studio → audience retention. Mark where people drop. A drop at a chapter start means a weak re-hook. A slow slide means the chapter is too long.
- Compare the drop near the midpoint twist with the drop in the rest of the film.
- Long films count toward the 4,000 public watch hours the Partner Program needs; Shorts views don't.
- Harvest 3–4 Shorts per long film, each with its own hook and "Related video" set to the long film.
